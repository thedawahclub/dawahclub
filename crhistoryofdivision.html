EARLY CHRISTIANITY (30-100 CE)

30-33 CE — Beginning of Christianity

Christianity emerged around 30-33 CE as a small Jewish movement centered around Jesus of Nazareth.

After Jesus’ ascension, the disciples convened, grappling with their beliefs and understanding of Jesus’ identity.

The Church's official history began with the Holy Spirit's descent at Pentecost, leading to missionary activities mainly directed at Jews and Gentiles.

Diverse early interpretations of Jesus' teachings led to different factions, especially regarding adherence to Jewish laws.

EARLY CHRISTIAN MOVEMENTS AND DIVISIONS

First Century CE — Jewish Christians, Pauline Christians and Gnostic Christians

By the end of the first century, three major movements had developed within Christianity:

(1) Jewish Christians
(2) Pauline Christians
(3) Gnostic Christians

The Jewish Christians followed Jesus while maintaining many Jewish traditions and laws.

Pauline Christians emphasized the inclusion of Gentiles and became the foundation of mainstream Christianity.

Gnostic groups developed different interpretations regarding Jesus, revelation and salvation.

Over time, Jewish Christianity declined due to Roman persecution and other historical pressures, while Pauline Christianity became dominant.

SECOND AND THIRD CENTURY DEVELOPMENTS

Christianity continued to expand throughout the Roman Empire, but disagreements over theology, doctrine and scripture resulted in many different movements.

Marcionism

Marcionism was an early Christian movement that emphasized a distinction between the God of the Old Testament and the God revealed through Jesus.

The movement rejected much of the Old Testament and developed its own collection of Christian writings.

Montanism

Montanism was an early Christian movement that emphasized prophecy, spiritual revelation and the continuing work of the Holy Spirit.

The movement was eventually rejected by mainstream Christianity.

Adoptionism

Adoptionism was a belief system that taught that Jesus was a special human chosen or adopted by God.

This view differed from later mainstream Christian teachings about the eternal nature of Christ.

Sabellianism

Sabellianism was a theological movement that addressed the relationship between the Father, Son and Holy Spirit.

It was rejected by mainstream Christianity because of disagreements regarding the nature of God.

These early movements were condemned by developing church authorities and contributed to further theological divisions within Christianity.

FOURTH CENTURY CHRISTIANITY

Early Fourth Century — Donatism

Donatism emerged in North Africa and was founded by Bishop Donatus Magnus.

The movement focused on whether sacraments performed by priests who had abandoned their faith during persecution were valid.

Donatists rejected the authority of restored apostate Christians and maintained that church leaders must remain pure.

The movement was declared heretical by mainstream Christianity and eventually disappeared by the seventh century.

Early Fourth Century — Arianism

Arianism was a major theological controversy associated with Arius.

Arius disputed the nature of Christ and taught that only the Father is God, while the Logos (pre-incarnate Jesus) was a created divine being.

This challenged the developing Trinitarian understanding of Christianity.

Arianism continued in some regions for centuries despite being rejected by mainstream Christianity.

CONSTANTINE AND THE COUNCIL OF NICAEA

312 CE — Conversion of Emperor Constantine

Constantine's conversion marked a major turning point in Christian history.

Until this period, Christians had often been a persecuted minority within the Roman Empire.

After Constantine's support, Christianity gained legal protection and eventually became the dominant religion of the empire.

325 CE — First Council of Nicaea

The First Council of Nicaea was called by Emperor Constantine to address disputes regarding Christian beliefs, especially concerning the nature of Jesus.

The council produced the Nicene Creed and established the foundation of Trinitarian Christianity.

Groups that rejected these teachings were increasingly considered outside accepted Christian belief.

380 CE — Christianity Becomes Official Religion of the Roman Empire

Christianity became the official religion of the Roman Empire under Emperor Theodosius I.

This brought greater unity and stability but also increased conflicts over doctrine, authority and church leadership.

FIFTH CENTURY DIVISIONS

431 CE — Nestorianism

Nestorianism developed from disagreements about the nature of Christ and the relationship between his divine and human natures.

The Council of Ephesus condemned Nestorian teachings, creating further separation among Christian communities.

Oriental Orthodox Church

The Oriental Orthodox Church emerged from disagreements following the Council of Chalcedon.

These churches rejected the Chalcedonian understanding of Christ's nature and followed a position known as Miaphysitism.

The Oriental Orthodox churches remain separate from both Roman Catholic and Eastern Orthodox Christianity.

THE GREAT SCHISM AND EAST-WEST DIVISION

1054 CE — The Great Schism

The Great Schism officially divided Christianity into two major branches:

(1) Roman Catholic Christianity
(2) Eastern Orthodox Christianity

The separation developed due to theological, cultural, political and practical disagreements.

Major disagreements included:

• The Filioque controversy regarding the procession of the Holy Spirit.
• The authority and leadership role of the Pope.
• Differences in worship practices and traditions.
• Differences concerning the Eucharist.
• Differences regarding clerical celibacy.

The division between Eastern and Western Christianity became even stronger after the Fourth Crusade and the sack of Constantinople in 1204 CE.

Despite multiple attempts at reconciliation, the separation remains largely unresolved.

MEDIEVAL CHRISTIAN MOVEMENTS

12th - 15th Century CE — Cathars

The Cathars emerged in Southern Europe during the medieval period.

They rejected many important Catholic teachings and developed their own understanding of Christianity.

The Catholic Church considered the movement heretical and launched campaigns against them.

By the early fifteenth century, the Cathar movement had disappeared due to persecution and decline.

12th Century CE — Waldensians

The Waldensian movement was founded by Peter Waldo.

The movement emphasized poverty, preaching and the authority of Scripture.

The Catholic Church eventually excommunicated the Waldensians and they faced persecution throughout Europe.

Many Waldensians later became associated with Protestant movements during the Reformation period.

14th Century CE — Lollards

The Lollards were followers of John Wycliffe.

They emphasized Scripture over church authority and promoted making the Bible available to ordinary people.

After Wycliffe's death in 1384, the movement was suppressed but influenced later reform movements.

15th Century CE — Hussites

The Hussite movement developed from the teachings of Jan Hus.

The movement called for church reform, wider access to communion and greater emphasis on Biblical interpretation.

Following Jan Hus' execution in 1415, his followers faced severe persecution but influenced later Protestant ideas.

THE PROTESTANT REFORMATION

1517 CE — Martin Luther and the Protestant Reformation

The Protestant Reformation began when Martin Luther challenged teachings and practices of the Roman Catholic Church.

His actions led to major religious and political changes throughout Europe.

The Reformation resulted in the creation of many Protestant denominations and permanently changed the Christian landscape.

1522 CE — Reformed Christianity

Reformed Christianity began through the work of Huldrych Zwingli in Switzerland.

It was further developed by John Calvin beginning in 1536.

Reformed Christians differed from Catholic teachings regarding church authority, worship practices and theology.

1534 CE — Anglicanism

The Anglican Church began when King Henry VIII separated the Church of England from the authority of the Roman Catholic Church.

The separation involved both religious and political reasons.

Anglicanism developed as a distinct Christian tradition containing elements from both Catholic and Protestant traditions.

GROWTH OF PROTESTANT DENOMINATIONS

Following the Protestant Reformation, many new Christian movements developed.

1606 CE — Baptists

The Baptist movement was founded by John Smyth.

Baptists emphasized believer's baptism, personal faith and the authority of Scripture.

17th Century CE — Quakers

The Religious Society of Friends, commonly known as Quakers, was established by George Fox around 1650.

Quakers emphasized direct spiritual experience, simplicity and personal guidance from God.

17th Century CE — Pietism

Pietism began within Lutheran Christianity.

It emphasized personal devotion, spiritual renewal and practical Christian living.

1738 CE — Methodism

Methodism developed through the work of John Wesley and Charles Wesley.

The movement emphasized personal holiness, evangelism and disciplined Christian living.

MODERN CHRISTIAN MOVEMENTS

18th - 19th Century CE — Episcopal Church

The Episcopal Church developed after the American Revolution as a continuation of Anglican Christianity in the United States.

1830 CE — Mormonism

The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints was founded by Joseph Smith in 1830.

The movement considers itself Christian, although many mainstream Christian groups classify it separately because of significant theological differences.

Mid-19th Century CE — Seventh-day Adventists

The Seventh-day Adventist movement developed from the Millerite movement.

It emphasizes Saturday Sabbath observance and teachings related to the second coming of Jesus.

Late 19th Century CE — Jehovah's Witnesses

Jehovah's Witnesses were established by Charles Taze Russell.

The movement developed distinctive beliefs that differ from mainstream Trinitarian Christianity.

1901 CE - Present — Pentecostalism

Pentecostalism began in the early twentieth century and gained worldwide attention following the Azusa Street Revival in 1906.

The movement emphasizes the gifts of the Holy Spirit, including speaking in tongues, healing and spiritual experiences.

FINAL SUMMARY

Christianity's 2000-year history showcases significant divisions into branches, sects and movements based on differences in theology, doctrine, ritual and church authority.

From its beginning as a Jewish movement in the first century, Christianity developed into a worldwide religion with many different traditions and interpretations.

Major historical developments include:

• Early Jewish Christianity and Pauline Christianity.
• Early theological movements and debates.
• Development of Trinitarian Christianity.
• The separation between Eastern Orthodox and Roman Catholic Christianity.
• The Protestant Reformation and creation of many Protestant denominations.
• The rise of modern Christian movements.
